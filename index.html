DispatchIQ: One AI That Knows Every Driver, Every Load, Every Dollar

An AI command center that picks the best driver, warns about trouble before it happens, and turns a photo of a delivery receipt into an invoice in 90 seconds.

Next.js · TypeScript · Groq LLM · Trucker Path NavPro · Google Maps · OpenWeather · TwilioContinued from A1

The problem

Fleet dispatchers weigh driver hours, location, cost and safety by hand, react to fuel, weather and fatigue problems after they happen, and spend around 45 minutes on the paperwork for each load.

What she built

  • Smart dispatch: Groq's llama-3.3-70b scores every driver on Hours-of-Service remaining, GPS proximity, cost per mile and safety, recommends the best one with its reasoning, creates the trip in NavPro and texts the driver.
  • Proactive alerts: watches fuel, HOS, severe weather and route deviation, finds the nearest truck stops, and sends SMS warnings before problems hit.
  • Cost intelligence: a custom dynamic-programming knapsack picks the most profitable combination of loads within weight and HOS limits, with mandatory FMCSA rest and fuel stops on the route.
  • Safety and compliance: a pre-dispatch FMCSA check blocks any dispatch with under 2 hours of HOS left, plus fatigue scoring and a full DOT audit log.
  • Billing autopilot: Groq Vision (llama-3.2-11b) reads bill-of-lading, delivery and receipt photos, extracts every field, generates the invoice and uploads it to NavPro.

The result

Ten API routes behind one dashboard, a demo mode that runs without any keys, and billing cut from 45 minutes to 90 seconds.

Teaching AI Assistants What to Forget

A controlled memory layer that decides what is worth remembering, retires preferences that changed, and matches vanilla RAG's accuracy with about a quarter of the memory.

Python · ChromaDB · sentence-transformers · BM25 · Groq (Llama 3.3 70B) · PersonaMemContinued from A1

The problem

Assistants that remember users usually store everything and retrieve by similarity. Memory bloats, and stale preferences come back: a user who said they like tea in turn 3 and switched to coffee in turn 40 gets both retrieved.

What the team built

  • Write router: extracts preference statements ("prefer", "don't like", "from now on") and normalizes them, so only what matters is stored.
  • Versioning: when a new memory conflicts with an old one, the old one is marked superseded and linked rather than deleted, so the history stays auditable.
  • Retrieval: only active memories are eligible; dense (MiniLM), BM25, option-conditioned and temporal signals are fused, then deduplicated.
  • Evaluation: one shared config compares no memory, summary-only, vanilla RAG and controlled memory on the PersonaMem v1 (32k) benchmark, with per-episode logs of every write and retrieval.

The result

On the full 588-episode evaluation, the best variant reached 66% accuracy while storing ~60% fewer memories than vanilla RAG, retiring an average of 7.8 outdated memories per episode. Co-authored with three teammates.

Clickless: Shopping for Headphones by Just Saying What You Want

"Wireless headphones under $100 with good bass for the gym." An agentic system turns that sentence into ranked, review-backed recommendations.

Python · OpenAI API · Streamlit · Streaming JSONL · UCSD Amazon Product Dataset

The problem

Shopping pages make people translate what they want into dozens of filters, then read thousands of reviews to trust a choice.

What she built

  • Planner → Retriever → Ranker → Explainer: the planner extracts budget, use case and priorities; the ranker scores budget fit, ratings, feature matches and use-case relevance; the explainer writes recommendations that cite real review pros and cons.
  • Conversational refinement: "make it wireless" or "increase budget to $150" keeps the earlier context and adjusts the search.
  • Data pipeline: streamed 25–30 GB of raw Amazon data into a compact index of products with aggregated review statistics.
  • Deterministic ranking around the LLM keeps recommendations grounded in real products.

The result

92% precision@50 and 93% budget-extraction accuracy.

Where Did the Forest Go? Mapping Amazon Deforestation From Space

About 6,800 hectares of natural forest cleared in Rondônia, Brazil, between 2021 and 2026, roughly 9,500 football fields, measured with validated uncertainty.

Python · PyTorch · U-Net · Sentinel-2 · MapBiomas · GeoPandas · Rasterio · FoliumContinued from A1

The question

News reports say the Amazon lost so many football fields of forest this year. Who is actually counting the trees? Sentinel-2 photographs the planet every few days for free, so she set out to do the counting herself, from raw satellite images to a map of exactly where the forest went, in a 55 × 55 km area of one of the most deforested places on Earth.

What she built

  • A pipeline from raw imagery: STAC search, cloud masking, dry-season median composites (July–August, when pasture and forest are easiest to tell apart) and brightness matching between years.
  • A U-Net trained from scratch on a Kaggle GPU, tested on squares from different parts of the map than it learned from, so the score is honest.
  • Change detection that counts a pixel as cleared only when the model is confident it was forest in 2021 and is not in 2026, turned into 926 clickable patches on an interactive map.

Three versions, two blind checks

Version 1 learned from ESA WorldCover and looked strong, but a blind hand check of 300 points showed it mistook crops, plantations and young regrowth for forest. Adding shortwave-infrared bands (version 2) changed nothing: the model was copying its labels. Version 3 changed only the labels, to MapBiomas, and a fresh blind check of 300 more points confirmed the fix: precision for cleared forest rose from 59% to 88%, at 92% overall accuracy.
